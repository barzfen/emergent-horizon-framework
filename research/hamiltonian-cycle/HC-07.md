# EHF-HC-07 · Cut sampling and compatible degree estimates

**PROVED.** A parity-aware incidence calculation gives an upper bound on the number of compatible child degree-profile pairs without building either exact child table.

**COMPUTATIONAL.** Sampling a few candidate cuts and scoring their boundary widths gave the fastest estimated total on eight of the ten [HC-06 workloads](HC-06.md#planning-overhead-and-candidate-joins). The degree estimate was loose on all 226 sampled cuts and ranked actual compatible record counts less accurately than the width score. These comparisons use the same exact solver and matching reduction in every timing configuration.

## Choosing fewer candidates

The all-start width planner from HC-06 tries a breadth-first edge order starting at every vertex of a piece and all split positions from one third to two thirds of its edge count. The sampled planner keeps the current edge order and two orders found by breadth-first sweeps. It starts at the smallest vertex, finds a farthest vertex, and uses the edge orders from that vertex and its farthest vertex. It tries just the first, middle and last allowable split positions, removing duplicates. This gives at most nine candidate cuts per piece. Both sampled variants consider the same candidate generator at any fixed piece.

The sampled width variant uses the same lexicographic width score as HC-06. The sampled degree variant tries to minimise the estimated number of compatible degree-profile pairs, using the raw profile-product estimate and then the width score to break ties. Each cut still defines an exact edge decomposition regardless of score.

## Counting a relaxation of the join

For a cut with children $L,R$, let $B_L,B_R$ be their boundaries and $B$ the parent's boundary. At each $v\in B_L\cup B_R$, write $d(v)$ for the path-degree requirement, one at a terminal and two otherwise. For a child in which $v$ is on the boundary, the maximum partial degree is the lesser of $d(v)$ and the number of available incident edges in that child. A vertex absent from that child's boundary contributes zero in this comparison. If it were internal to that child, it could have no incidence in the other child and would be absent from the active union.

Allow the two partial degrees $a,b$ to vary independently between zero and their maxima before enforcing these conditions.

1. For $v\notin B$, require $a+b=d(v)$ since $v$ is being forgotten. For $v\in B$, require $a+b\le d(v)$, the degree test of the exact join.
2. The sum of boundary degrees in each child must be even. Internal vertices have degree two and a selected edge contributes two to the full degree sum. The underlying parity constraint is also used in matching-based Hamiltonian dynamic programming. [Cygan, Kratsch and Nederlof (2018), §4](https://doi.org/10.1145/3148227)

For each vertex count the allowed local pairs by their two parities. Convolve the counts over the four child parity combinations, starting at even-even. The final even-even entry is the relaxed compatible count. Every actual compatible pair of degree profiles satisfies these conditions, so it cannot exceed the count. The argument counts distinct profile pairs. Several different matchings can carry the same profile, so their record multiplicities require separate measurement.

Dropping the first condition gives the raw profile-product estimate. For one child with maxima $c_v$, its number of even assignments is $(\prod_v(c_v+1)+\prod_v\sum_{i=0}^{c_v}(-1)^i)/2$. Multiplying the two child counts gives the raw estimate. Both scores use only the incidence counts and a constant number of parity counters per vertex.

### A four-vertex overcount

Take terminals $1,3$, left edges $02,03$, and right edges $13,23$. Vertex $0$ is internal to the left piece and must use both its edges. The left profile on $(2,3)$ is therefore $(1,1)$. The estimate admits the right profile $(1,1,0)$ on $(1,2,3)$. It has even sum, fits the separate incidence bounds, and would give the forgotten vertex $2$ degree two. Both right edges are incident to $3$, so giving $1$ and $2$ degree one would use both edges and give $3$ degree two. The right profile is impossible. The verifier finds one compatible pair in the relaxation and zero in the actual join.

## Results

The four planners ran on the same ten terminal queries from HC-06 with compact matching reduction. All decisions agreed with the independent whole-graph oracle. The table gives the sum of the median planning and solving CPU times over nine runs in milliseconds, on CPython 3.12.14 and an AMD EPYC 9V74. The stages were timed separately and matching caches were cleared before each solve.

For the 24-vertex grid, sampled widths kept the same maximum boundary of five and cut planning time from 7.230 to 1.311 ms. On $K_9$, the all-start width plan tried 98,720 record pairs, while the two sampled plans tried 125,168 and 116,986. The saved planning time there was smaller than the extra solve time. Degree compatibility is only one stage of the join. On $K_{5,5}$, sampled width tried 3,166 record pairs, of which 719 passed the degree check. All-start width tried 6,284, of which 477 passed. The present solver scans candidate pairs before checking degrees, so the cheaper total tracked the smaller attempted count in that case.

### How well do the scores order cuts?

The diagnostic enumerates the sampled candidates at the root and its first two child pieces, holding each parent piece fixed and computing the exact child tables without representative reduction. Across 226 candidates in 30 pieces, the compatible estimate was never tight. Among positive actual compatible profile counts, the median ratio of estimate to actual was 7.714. The verifier checked the two upper bounds against the relevant distinct-profile counts on every cut.

For every pair of candidates within a piece with unequal actual counts, award one point for the correct order and half a point for a predicted tie. Width means the full HC-06 width score. The degree score includes the same tie-breakers as the sampled degree planner. All percentages are measured on the same candidate pairs for a target.

| Actual target | Candidate comparisons | Width score | Raw profile product | Compatible estimate | Degree score |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Compatible pairs of distinct profiles | 363 | 71.21% | 69.01% | 69.15% | 69.97% |
| Compatible pairs of full records | 359 | 72.98% | 72.01% | 66.85% | 70.61% |
| All attempted pairs of full records | 739 | 83.90% | 84.64% | 74.90% | 78.21% |

The raw profile product narrowly led the ranking of attempted joins. Applying the compatible-degree constraint to the relaxation gave a worse predictor on this suite. The four-vertex example shows one source of overcount, and the table uses actual path records to show the effect on cut ordering. The next test is to group actual records by degree so that the join can skip incompatible groups before examining their matchings.

## Reproduce

Run from the repository root with Python 3.12 and its standard library. The [verifier](verify_hc_07.py) includes the four-vertex control, the timed runs, and the exact candidate audit. This probe was developed with AI assistance. Its timings and ordering scores apply to the stated workloads, candidate generator and solver.

```sh
python3 research/hamiltonian-cycle/verify_hc_07.py --repeats 9
```
